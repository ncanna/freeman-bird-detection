\begin{abstract}
Object detectors answer \emph{where}; the emerging class of \emph{\sone{}
decision models} answers \emph{typed questions}: a state (optionally with
media) plus a map of closed questions returns calibrated probability
distributions in one forward pass with zero generated tokens. Our target
detection problem is the Hume's Leaf Warbler (\emph{Phylloscopus humei}) on
Freeman camera-trap footage: a small ($\approx$10\,cm), cryptically plumaged
leaf warbler that occupies a small fraction of the frame and is frequently
occluded by vegetation --- the hard tail of the detection distribution, across
a growing collection of site datasets of which H55 is one. We report an
implementation study of wiring a decision model (Jev-Omni, a Gemma-4-12B
unified multimodal transformer with a trained linear decision head) into our
pipeline, which otherwise uses supervised detectors (YOLO, RT-DETR). We
contribute: (i) a harness that maps COCO box annotations onto three typed
questions and maps answers back into valid COCO output; (ii) a reproducible
video-sampling and frame-extraction protocol; (iii) an empirical finding that
the released checkpoint's calibrated probabilities are unusable at standard
thresholds on our domain (230/230 frames called ``no bird'' with mean
confidence 0.897; maximum bird probability 0.274 across the run); (iv) a
dissection of the decision-model architecture showing it is a fine-tuned VLM
whose adaptation recipe is published inside the artifact; and (v) a tiered
fine-tuning roadmap from threshold refitting through head retraining to LoRA
adaptation, with the data protocol required to execute it. This document
doubles as the engineering record for the implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\paragraph{Study system.} The Georgia Tech Mountain Bird Lab monitors bird
activity at the Freeman site with motion-triggered camera traps. The target
species is the Hume's Leaf Warbler (\emph{Phylloscopus humei}), a
$\approx$10\,cm cryptically plumaged leaf warbler: drab against bark and leaf
litter, often foraging inside vegetation, and therefore --- at typical
camera-trap standoff distances --- a small, partially occluded object that is
difficult even for human annotators to spot (in the H55 sample studied here,
several frames we initially read as containing a bird were judged bird-free by
the site owner). The footage arrives as a growing set of
site datasets --- h23 (annotated with COCO boxes, the training corpus for our
supervised detectors), h03, and the H55 download studied here --- so methods
must be multi-site by construction: seeded sampling and extraction run
per dataset, and any model we adapt must cope with site-specific burn-ins
(every H55 frame carries a Browning info bar stamped \code{H55}) without
per-site curation.

Automated camera-trap pipelines overwhelmingly treat ``what is in this
frame?'' as an object-detection problem. Our repository follows that
convention: \pkg{hlwdetector} trains YOLO11/26 and RT-DETR adapters on COCO
boxes extracted from Freeman-site footage (the h23 dataset), with per-video
train/val/test splits, mAP-style evaluation, and confusion-matrix tooling.
Detection, however, is not the only useful output. Field ecology often asks
coarser questions first --- \emph{is any bird visible?}, \emph{roughly how
many?}, \emph{where in the frame?} --- and for a species the size and habits
of \emph{P.~humei}, those coarser questions are the difference between a
usable screening tool and nothing at all: if box-level detection is the hard
part, a calibrated ``is a warbler plausibly in this frame?'' is still worth
having. A model that answers such questions \emph{with calibrated
probabilities, without generating text}, is attractive on three axes: no box
supervision is required to ask them; the answers compose into COCO-format
artifacts cheaply; and the interface is auditable (a distribution over a
closed option set, not free text).

The \sone{} model family (TypeSafe Jev, Laya, Jev-Omni, OmniJev, and
compatible servers) exposes exactly this interface: a \emph{state} plus a map
of questions, each of type \code{noul} (yes/no probability), \code{choice}
(distribution over a caller-defined option set), or \code{score} (an ordered
rubric). This paper documents the first end-to-end exercise of that interface
on our data: a reproducible, seeded subsample of the H55 site (5 of 1{,}144
videos, 230 extracted frames), a harness (\pkg{system\_one\_detector}) that
runs any of nine registered backends over COCO-adjacent frame sets, conversion
of per-frame answers into COCO format, and visualization of the result. H55 is
deliberately treated as a template: every tool here is dataset-agnostic and
re-runs unchanged on the other site datasets as they enter the pipeline.

Two findings organize everything that follows. First, \emph{the decision
model's released calibration does not transfer to our domain}: the model
assigns ``bird present'' a maximum probability of 0.274 across 230 frames
while never exceeding a 0.5-class decision, so threshold-based consumption of
its answers is empty by construction. Second, \emph{the model is a fine-tuned
VLM}, and its fine-tuning recipe ships inside the artifact --- which makes
domain adaptation not merely possible but a matter of executing a documented
process at reduced scale.

\section{Two paradigms in one repository}
\label{sec:paradigms}

The repository deliberately maintains both paradigms side by side.

\paragraph{Supervised detection (the DETR side).}
\pkg{hlwdetector} wraps Ultralytics YOLO11/26 and RT-DETR as registered
adapters driven by YAML experiment configs. The production configuration
(\code{rtdetr\_h23\_full.yaml}) fine-tunes \code{rtdetr-l.pt} for 100 epochs
at $640^2$, batch 32, on gold COCO boxes from the h23 split. Every gradient
step optimizes the target task on in-domain imagery; evaluation produces
mAP50/mAP50-95, precision/recall, and frame-level confusion matrices with
TP/FP/TN/FN example mining. This is the performance ceiling of the
repository, and its mechanism is unremarkable by design: supervised
fine-tuning on labeled in-domain data with a measurement loop.

\paragraph{Typed decisions (the \sone{} side).}
\pkg{system\_one\_detector} is a self-contained package (not an
\pkg{hlwdetector} adapter) that asks typed questions of frames. Its backend
registry covers nine adapters: TypeSafe Jev (text-only), Laya (text-only),
Jev-Omni (image/audio/video, CUDA), Cloudflare Clef (images), Djev
(diffusion-sampled, invite-only), \code{jevimage} (dual-encoder embeddings),
OmniJev v1.1 (Qwen3.5 LoRA + decision heads), an OpenAI Decisions adapter, and
a generic HTTP adapter for OneJev-style \code{/v1/systemone} servers. Text-only
backends double as metadata baselines; pixel-seeing backends receive the PNG
frame. The harness enforces interface discipline (media-type guards before
credential checks), one \code{decide()} call per frame, per-frame error
capture, and Brier/ECE/accuracy scoring against gold labels where they exist.

The two sides answer different questions. The detector answers ``where is the
bird'' with boxes; the decision model answers ``is there a bird, how many,
whereabouts'' with distributions. Section~\ref{sec:coco} shows the answers
compose into COCO; Section~\ref{sec:finetune} shows the decision model can be
pushed toward the detector's training regime.

\subsection{Positioning against two recent threads}
\label{sec:threads}

Two contemporaneous papers bound this work from both sides of the token
bottleneck, and both are NVIDIA-led.

\paragraph{Encoder-free understanding (PixelUMM; input side).}
PixelUMM \cite{pixelumm2026} removes the vision encoder and VAE altogether:
images are $16\times16$ raw-pixel patches, videos are spatiotemporal tubelets,
joined to a Qwen3-8B backbone by single-layer linear projections through a
Mixture-of-Transformers (understanding expert, generation expert, shared
self-attention), trained with autoregressive text prediction and pixel-space
flow matching. Its claim that matters to us is \emph{understanding at native
pixel resolution}: no discrete vision-token budget stands between the sensor
and the language model. Our calibration failure
(Section~\ref{sec:results}) leaves open whether the bird is ever resolved by
the vision tower at all --- at 70 image tokens, a $\approx$10\,cm warbler at
camera-trap standoff may fall below the encoder's effective resolution.
PixelUMM-class backbones are the architectural endpoint of the
high-token-budget and tile-crop tests we propose: if detail, not reasoning,
is the bottleneck, encoder-free native-resolution understanding attacks it
directly, and our decision head --- which reads only the last hidden state
--- is backbone-portable by construction.

\paragraph{Parallel Box Decoding (LocateAnything; output side).}
LocateAnything \cite{locateanything2026} attacks the output side: VLM
grounding conventionally serializes each box into 1D coordinate tokens
decoded autoregressively, fragmenting box geometry and capping throughput.
Parallel Box Decoding (PBD) instead treats each box as an \emph{atomic
block} --- four quantized coordinates plus structural tokens, decoded in one
parallel step via box-aligned multi-token prediction, co-trained with a
standard NTP formulation to preserve causal reasoning, with a
hybrid mode that falls back to token-wise decoding on unreliable blocks. It
reports $2$--$6\times$ decoding speedups and better high-IoU localization
than token-serialized grounding at matched scale, trained on 138M queries.
PBD is the natural Tier-3 extension of our interface: our 256-way head is
already a parallel discrete decoder (all option probabilities in one pass,
zero generated tokens), and PBD generalizes the same principle from verdicts
to geometry. Under our typed-question contract, a box question would become
\emph{another atomic unit type} ---$noul$/$choice$/$score$ coins plus a
\code{box} oracle emitting $(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)$ in one step --- replacing the
$3\times3$ cell prior with real boxes, and upgrading \code{bird\_count} from
a coarse rubric to per-bird boxes in dense frames. Its explicit
\emph{Negative block} is our \code{bird\_present} gate, rediscovered.

Both threads support this paper's organizing claim: probabilistic decision
interfaces and supervised detection are converging on the same regime ---
supervised fine-tuning of an LLM backbone on structured targets, with
calibration shipped alongside the weights.

\section{Harness design}
\label{sec:harness}

\subsection{From COCO boxes to typed questions}
\label{sec:questions}

Three default questions map COCO box annotations onto the \sone{} interface:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{bird\_present} (\code{noul}) --- ``Is at least one bird visible
        in this camera-trap frame?'' Gold: $1$ iff the frame has $\ge 1$ box.
  \item \code{bird\_count} (\code{score}) --- ordered rubric over
        none\,/\,one\,/\,2--3\,/\,4+. Gold: the count level of the frame's boxes.
  \item \code{largest\_bird\_cell} (\code{choice}) --- the center of the
        largest bird (by box area) as one of nine $3\times3$ grid cells, plus
        an explicit \code{none} option (ten options total, deliberately under
        the $\sim$20-option degradation reported for this family). Gold: the
        cell containing the largest box's center.
\end{itemize}

The text state carries only the frame name, video stem, and frame index ---
never label-derived facts --- with an explicit note that the text does not
describe the picture. A fourth question (\code{primary\_category}) activates
only when the COCO category list plus \code{none}/\code{other} stays at or
below 20 options.

\subsection{Engine and outputs}

The engine loads a split (COCO JSON, split JSON, or a flat images directory),
builds the question map once, and calls the backend once per frame, recording
answers, usage, and per-frame errors without failing the run. Outputs land in
\code{outputs/<config>\_<timestamp>/} as \code{decisions.json} (normalized
answers per frame) and \code{metrics.json}. A mock backend with deterministic
answers supports dry runs that exercise the full path --- split filtering,
question building, scoring --- without weights, keys, or a GPU.

\subsection{From answers back to COCO}
\label{sec:coco}

Because \sone{} models emit no coordinates, the COCO mapping must not invent
them. Our converter (\code{tools/coco\_from\_decisions.py}) therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item emits one COCO \emph{image} entry per frame (an empty annotation list
        is a prediction of ``no bird''), with the raw answers preserved in an
        \code{attributes} extension;
  \item emits an \emph{annotation} only when (a) $P(\text{bird}) \ge \tau$
        (default $\tau = 0.5$) \emph{and} (b) the cell answer names a real
        grid cell (not \code{none});
  \item sets \code{bbox} to the \emph{predicted cell rectangle} --- exactly one
        ninth of the frame, positioned by the cell --- a location prior rather
        than a tight box;
  \item sets \code{score} to the bird-present probability, and preserves the
        full cell and count distributions in \code{attributes};
  \item records provenance (input sha256, threshold, model identities) in
        \code{info.source}.
\end{enumerate}

Contradictory frames (bird present but cell \code{none}) produce no annotation
rather than a guessed box. The mapping is honest by construction, which turns
out to matter: Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the $\tau=0.5$ export is empty
for the released checkpoint, and the honest reading of the $\tau=0.2$ export
is ``regions to review,'' not detections.

\section{The H55 experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Data}

H55 is one of several site datasets in the program. The corpus as of this
study:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{h23} --- the annotated main dataset (COCO boxes extracted from
        video, per-video splits); the training and evaluation corpus for the
        supervised detectors. Located on the cluster; not on the analysis
        laptop.
  \item \textbf{h03} --- a second camera-trap dataset, not yet exercised by
        the \sone{} harness.
  \item \textbf{H55} --- the raw download studied here:
        \code{data/H55/Download1\_28072024/} holds 1{,}144 H.264 videos
        ($1280\times720$, 29.97\,fps, mostly 10\,s / 303 frames, $\sim$5.9\,MB
        each; some clips run to 35\,s) and 1{,}144 camera-generated
        \code{*\_QV.JPG} thumbnails (unused), 7.4\,GB total. Every frame

\end{itemize}

There are no annotations of any kind for H55 at the time of writing;
researcher-provided presence labels for a subset are being downloaded and will
become the first gold set (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The model declines to see birds --- everywhere}

The bird-present probability never reaches 0.5: the maximum is \textbf{0.274}
(\code{IMG\_0572\_frame\_000700}), followed by 0.212, 0.208, 0.202, 0.199;
the distribution is a long low tail (median $\approx 0.09$, minimum 0.064).
The count question agrees (trivially correlated with presence). The cell
question, asked unconditionally by the POC, is uninformative without a bird:
its confidence averages 0.280 and its argmax spreads over five cells
(middle-right 108, top-left 55, center 30, middle-left 28, top-center 9).

This matches a prior 11-frame pilot on \code{train\_images} (11/11 ``no bird''
at $\approx$0.91 confidence): the shape is a property of the
checkpoint-on-this-domain pairing, not of one sample. Given that the site
owner judges the top-ranked frames bird-free --- the birds on this site are
camouflaged and small in frame --- the model's \emph{negatives} may be largely
correct; what fails is \emph{calibration}: the probability scale is
systematically compressed far below any usable threshold.

The study species sharpen this reading into a testable hypothesis. A
$\approx$10\,cm warbler at camera-trap standoff occupies on the order of tens
of pixels in a $1280\times720$ frame; at the 70-token image budget used here,
the effective per-token resolution plausibly renders the bird sub-threshold
for the vision tower, in which case no calibration could recover a signal the
encoder never resolves. Two cheap falsification tests follow from the
harness: re-run at the 280-token budget (the measured 22\,s/frame cost is
acceptable on a screening pass), and tile-crop ensembling (four half-res
crops, one decision each, aggregated). If the compressed-scale behaviour
persists at high token budget and on crops, the failure is representational
and points at Tier 3 (Section~\ref{sec:finetune}); if it lifts, it is an
input-budget problem and Tier 1 suffices.

Consistent with the documented noul bias of this model family (OmniJev v1.1
ships a learned $\text{noul bias} = 0.0531$ for exactly this reason), the
released temperature, fit on JevBench, is a benchmark artifact, not a domain
artifact.

\subsection{COCO exports at two thresholds}

At $\tau = 0.5$ the COCO export contains 230 image entries and zero
annotations --- a valid COCO dataset asserting ``no birds anywhere,'' which is
the honest representation of the run. A sensitivity export at $\tau = 0.2$
yields four annotations, all on \code{IMG\_0572} (cells tl, tl, mc, mc; scores
0.202--0.274).

\subsection{Visualization}

\code{tools/coco\_draw.py} renders COCO annotations back onto frames with the
$3\times3$ grid overlaid (essential context, since these boxes \emph{are}
cells), labels each box with $P(\text{bird})$, cell confidence, and count
rubric, and builds a montage contact sheet. The four rendered boxes sit on
vegetation texture; none contains a confirmed bird. Two readings follow: the
threshold-0.2 survivors are texture false positives from a single video (so
the sample provides no independent evidence), and cell-level localization from
the released head is not yet meaningful on this domain.

\section{What the decision model actually is}
\label{sec:architecture}

The local MLX snapshot and the upstream artifact pin the architecture down
exactly.

\paragraph{Base.} \code{google/gemma-4-12B-it}: a 48-layer text backbone
(hidden size 3840) with separate vision and audio towers, exposed as
\code{Gemma4UnifiedForConditionalGeneration} (\code{model\_type:
gemma4\_unified}). Jev-Omni is tagged \code{merged} --- it \emph{is} a
fine-tune: ``Built on Gemma 4 12B IT, with a 30{,}000-question fine-tuning
run.''

\paragraph{Decision head.} A prompt is assembled from state, question, and
numbered options; one forward pass produces the sequence; the \textbf{last
hidden state} is normalized by stored feature statistics ($\mu,\sigma$,
each $1\times3840$), projected by a linear head
$\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{256\times3840}$ sliced to the option count $K$,
plus bias, then softmaxed and temperature-scaled:
\begin{equation}
p \;=\; \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{h_{\text{last}} - \mu}{\sigma}\,\mathbf{W}_{[:K]}^{\top} + b_{[:K]}
\right)^{1/T}.
\end{equation}
There is no vocabulary projection and no token generation: the ``System One''
interface is a classification head riding on a multimodal LLM. Measured from
\code{decision\_head/weights.safetensors}: \textbf{990{,}976 FP32 parameters
total}. In the MLX conversion the LM weights are 4-bit affine (group 64),
vision weights remain BF16, and the head remains FP32. The head is sliced
\emph{positionally}, so option order is part of the interface contract.

\paragraph{The fine-tuning recipe ships in the artifact.} The upstream
\code{decision\_config.json} records how the released weights were produced:
LoRA rank 512, $\alpha{=}512$, learning rate $10^{-5}$ (head too), 24{,}000
records, one epoch (750 steps), effective batch 32, linear warmup (10\%) then
decay to 10\% of peak, 4-GPU DDP, $\approx$2.1B trainable parameters,
initialized from ``FP32 merged trained v1 + trained head + fresh LoRA'' ---
iterative merge-then-adapt rounds. The ecosystem replicates the recipe across
backbones: OmniJev v1.1 is the same design (LoRA + decision heads + an
ordinal head) on Qwen3.5 in three sizes.

\section{Fine-tuning roadmap}
\label{sec:finetune}

The supervised side of the repository improves by unremarkable means ---
in-domain labels, a task-aligned loss, and a measurement loop
(Section~\ref{sec:paradigms}). The decision model can be pushed down the same
path in three tiers of increasing cost.

\subsection{Tier 1 --- refit the calibration (no training)}

Fit an operating threshold $\tau$ and per-question temperature on gold frames.
This converts the run's ranking behaviour into decisions with a measured error
rate. Hours of work; no gradients. It also partially absorbs the constant
info-bar effect (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\subsection{Tier 2 --- retrain the decision head (this Mac)}

Because \code{omni\_mlx/classifier.py} already extracts $h_{\text{last}}$,
feature capture is one forward pass per (frame, question) --- the inference we
already run, plus a 3{,}840-dim vector write-out. The head is then a
regularized multinomial logistic regression over frozen features:
$\sim$991k parameters, minutes to train. Training on \emph{4-bit-extracted}
features exactly matches the deployment path. Head retraining also learns
around the info bar: it is a constant visual feature across frames, and a
trained head can encode it as context rather than noise. Data requirement:
hundreds to a few thousand labeled frames; splits must be by \emph{video}, not
by frame.

\subsection{Tier 3 --- LoRA on the backbone (GPUs)}

The published recipe (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) scales down: rank
16--128, lr $10^{-5}$, one epoch over 1--5k in-domain records, with a replay
mix to avoid forgetting the benchmark alignment. This requires the upstream
BF16 checkpoint and CUDA (the repository's Slurm/submitit patterns apply); the
4-bit MLX conversion is an inference artifact, not a training starting point.
Beyond Tier 3, the output contract itself can grow a geometry primitive: a
box-aligned block head in the style of Parallel Box Decoding
\cite{locateanything2026} would emit real coordinates atomically, replacing
the $3\times3$ cell prior (Section~\ref{sec:threads}).
\pkg{mlx-vlm} 0.7.4 also ships an SFT/LoRA trainer with a vision-language loss
and a \code{gemma4\_unified} implementation --- training on Apple Silicon is
plausible but untested for this architecture.

\paragraph{Order of operations.} Head-only first: it is matched to deployment
and cheap; if it stalls, that is the signal that the \emph{features} (not the
probe) are the bottleneck and Tier 3 is worth GPU spend. Following the OmniJev
lesson, any artifact we train ships with its own bias/calibration file ---
fine-tuned heads shift decision boundaries.

\section{Ongoing and planned work}
\label{sec:roadmap}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Gold labels.} Researchers hand-annotated a subset of H55
        frames with bird presence (a positive/negative mix); these become the
        first true evaluation set for \code{bird\_present} and
        \code{bird\_count}, and the basis for Tier 1--2. Presence-only labels
        do not supervise \code{largest\_bird\_cell}; cell training requires
        location boxes.
  \item \textbf{Temporal aggregation.} Frames are stride-10 samples of
        continuous video; a bird visible for 2\,s spans $\sim$60 sampled
        neighbours. Aggregating decisions per video (max or median
        $P(\text{bird})$ over a video's frames) converts temporal redundancy
        into recall for free, and per-frame gold will tell us which
        aggregation wins rather than guessing.
  \item \textbf{The info bar stays.} We deliberately do not crop the
        burned-in footer; the model must learn our deployment distribution.
        Its effect is systematic (constant across frames), which calibration
        and head retraining absorb; a per-cell prior correction remains
        available if bottom-row cells show a directional bias.
  \item \textbf{Detector cross-check.} Running the h23-trained YOLO/RT-DETR
        adapters over the same 230 frames gives an agreement analysis whose
        disagreements are the highest-value frames to label next (active
        learning).
  \item \textbf{From presence to species.} The program end goal is detection
        \emph{of the Hume's Leaf Warbler}, not of birds in general. The
        harness already supports a \code{primary\_category} choice question
        when the category list stays under 20 options; with the h23 category
        vocabulary (or a warbler-centric reduction:
        \code{hume's leaf warbler} / \code{other bird} / \code{none}), the
        same typed interface extends from ``is a bird present'' to ``is this
        the warbler,'' and gold presence labels plus site records can
        supervise it. The head's positional option slicing
        (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}) makes canonical option order part of
        the training contract.
  \item \textbf{Multi-site generalization.} Every tool here is
        dataset-agnostic; h03 and future downloads re-run the
        sample$\to$extract$\to$decide$\to$COCO loop unchanged. Site-specific
        burn-ins (the H55 info bar) argue for including all sites in the
        fine-tuning mixture so no single site's overlay becomes a learned
        crutch, and for reporting per-site calibration separately.
  \item \textbf{Input budget.} The image-token budget trades detail for
        latency (70 tokens: 1.5--9\,s per decision locally; 280 tokens: up to
        22\,s). A $\approx$10\,cm warbler at standoff argues for the high end;
        tile-crop ensembling is the classical small-object fallback and
        composes with any backend. The 280-token re-run on the same 230
        frames is the immediate next experiment. The newest member of the
        family changes this budget's economics: Liquid's d1-3B
        \cite{liquid2026d1} --- a decision model on the LFM2.5-VL-3B backbone,
        released 2026-10-07 with open weights --- answers a 384px image in
        62\,ms on laptop-class hardware, which makes dense whole-dataset
        sampling and tile ensembles cheap enough to run routinely.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Threats to validity}

\textbf{No gold labels at write time.} Every number in Section~\ref{sec:results}
is model behaviour, not accuracy; the site owner's judgment that the top
frames are bird-free is expert opinion on a hard (camouflaged) domain, itself
error-prone. \textbf{Sample size and dependence.} 230 frames from 5 videos;
all four threshold survivors come from one video, so nothing is independent
evidence. \textbf{Quantization.} The 4-bit conversion reports JevBench macro
85.90\% (micro 87.88\%) versus upstream merged-model 86.15\%/87.45\% on the
matched set --- close, but the conversion card itself warns the numbers are
not directly comparable. \textbf{Single checkpoint.} Findings are about
Jev-Omni-on-H55, not the \sone{} family; the text-only baselines (Jev, Laya)
were excluded by design here since the task is visual.

\section{Conclusion}

A decision model plugs into a camera-trap pipeline cleanly at both ends: COCO
boxes become typed questions, and typed answers become valid (if honest) COCO.
What does not transfer, today, is calibration: the released artifact's
probabilities are compressed far below any usable threshold on our domain,
which empties threshold-based consumption and turns ``what is possible''
experiments into calibration studies. For the program's target --- detecting a
$\approx$10\,cm cryptic warbler in vegetation --- this is expected rather than
surprising, and the architecture dissection explains why it is fixable rather
than fundamental: the model is a fine-tuned VLM whose own recipe (LoRA +
decision head + calibration artifacts) is published and scalable down to our
data regime, with a head-only tier that runs on the same machine that runs
inference. The near-term plan is deliberately unglamorous: gold labels,
threshold and temperature refitting, temporal aggregation, a detector
cross-check, and the high-token-budget re-run that tests whether the bird is
resolved at all --- the same three levers, labels, loss alignment, and
measurement, that carry the DETR side, aimed at a species-sized question.
